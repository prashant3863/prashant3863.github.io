Scope and change · weeks 4 to 8Prashant Mittal, realfast

About twenty minutes. Land the Friends joke, then move on.

Every Friends episode is called "The One Where…" or "The One With…". You never remember the plot, only the one thing that happened. This is the one about scope creep.

Session one was reading the SoW. Session two was estimating the work. Once the project is signed and running, the estimate meets the requests. That starts from about week four, when the requests start to arrive. They are small, reasonable, and asked in passing at the end of a call.

Here's the idea I want you to leave with. Managing scope isn't saying no, and it isn't absorbing every yes. It's owning the date, and making every yes show what it costs while the request is still small.

We are consultants and forward-deployed engineers. We sit with the client every week. We hear each ask first. So managing scope is our job, and when scope creep moves the date, that slip is ours.

Part 1 · Why scope grows

Scope grows because the work reveals it.

Scope creep isn’t the fault of bad clients, bad managers, or bad programmers.
Ryan Singer · Shape Up, Basecamp, 2019

You learn what the software needs by building it. The client learns what they want by seeing it.

The one about scope creep · Session 32 / 18

Take the blame out of the room before the method.

Part one: why scope grows at all. This line is from Shape Up, the book Basecamp wrote about how they run product work. The next sentence in the book is "Projects are opaque at the macro scale." You can't see all the work from the start.

That's true on every project we run. Your team learns what the software needs by building it. The client learns what they want by seeing a working screen. Both of those are good things. A project where nobody learns anything new by week five is a project where nobody is paying attention.

So expect scope to move on every project. You can't stop it, and you shouldn't try. What you control is whether both teams see it move, and who decides what each move costs.

The one about scope creep · Session 33 / 18

The first way a yes slips through.

In 1966 two researchers asked homeowners in California to put a large, badly made "Drive Carefully" sign on their front lawn. Asked cold, 17% agreed. A second group had been asked two weeks earlier to put up a small three-inch "Be a safe driver" sign. When the same people were asked about the big sign, 76% agreed.

The authors' explanation: after the first yes, the person becomes, in their own eyes, "the kind of person who does this sort of thing." Later reviews found the effect is smaller and less steady than in this one study. Treat it as a nudge, not magic.

That nudge is how creep works on our teams. After you've said "sure, we'll squeeze it in" four times, the fifth one feels normal. The client learns the same thing from the other side: asking works, so they ask. Neither side is doing anything wrong. Nobody stopped to add it up.

The one about scope creep · Session 34 / 18

The hardest ask to handle, because the client believes it. Call back to session one.

The second way scope grows is the one people get angry about: "it was always included." The example is made up, but you've all seen one like it.

Week four, the first milestone demo. It goes well. Then the sponsor says: the SoW says reporting, so when do we see the dashboard? We always meant the dashboard.

The SoW does say "reporting". We read it as three exports. They read it as a live dashboard. Both are fair readings of one word.

The easy answer is to argue about what the SoW meant. Nobody wins that argument, and it sours the next eight weeks.

Here's what you do instead. Open the SoW ledger from session one, where that line was already flagged as vague. Say: "I can see why you read it that way. We read it as exports. Let's price the dashboard and decide." The dashboard is eight days. Bulk edit, which is in scope, is also eight days. Ask which matters more. The sponsor picks the dashboard and moves bulk edit to a later phase. The date doesn't move. It took a decision, not an argument.

Kent Beck and Dave Cleal described this in 1999. On a fixed-scope contract, the client reads each requirement as broadly as it can be read, and the supplier reads it as narrowly as it can be read. Neither side is acting in bad faith. The contract sets them up to read it differently.

The one about scope creep · Session 35 / 18

Added after the first run, from the questions. Keep it short: this is its own session later.

When I ran this session the first time, the most common question was this one: clients often don't know what they want until they see something built. So how do we find out earlier?

You can't remove the opacity, but you can move the surprises earlier, where they're cheap. Three habits help.

Show a prototype. A clickable mock, or a demo of the screen, lets the client react before anyone writes the code. Mockups don't catch everything, but they catch the dashboard-versus-exports kind of gap from the last slide.

Watch the work today. Sit with the people who'll use the software, in the tools they use now, and watch how they do the job. Their real process gives you better questions than any document does. Once you understand how it works today, designing the new version is the easier part.

Ask the five Ws. Who, what, when, where, why and how. Clients know what they need. They find it hard to say, and pulling it out is our job. Don't treat requirements as a box to tick in week one.

Some back and forth during the build is still normal. What you're avoiding is the big turn in month three.

Part 2 · Push back as a partner

Pushing back is what a partner does.

A vendor
  • Takes the list of features
  • Says yes to keep things warm
  • Explains the slip at the end
A partner
  • Asks what problem it solves
  • Shows what the yes costs
  • Keeps the date
Why it worksMarty Cagan, “Product vs. Feature Teams”, SVPG (2019): feature teams take a roadmap of features; product teams are trusted to solve problems. We’re hired to be the second kind.
The one about scope creep · Session 36 / 18

Give permission. Pushing back is normal and expected.

Part two. Scope will grow, so you will push back, often. How you stand when you do it matters. A lot of first-time delivery managers feel that pushing back on a client is rude, or risky, or above their pay grade. It's none of those. It's the reason the client hired a partner and not a vendor.

Marty Cagan, who writes about product teams, draws the line this way. A feature team gets handed a list of features, a roadmap, and builds it. A product team gets handed a problem and is trusted to find the best way to solve it. Clients come to us for judgement. If we behave like a feature team, we are easy to replace.

Look at the left column. Each of those feels kind in the moment. Saying yes keeps the call pleasant. But the bill arrives at the end, as a slip, and you explain it after the fact.

The right column feels harder in the moment and is kinder over the project. You ask what the request is for. You show what it costs. You keep the date. On a healthy project you'll push back several times a month. That's part of the job, and nobody needs to treat it as conflict.

Part 2 · Push back as a partner

Look for the win-win.

Why it worksMary Parker Follett’s parable, retold in Fisher and Ury, Getting to Yes. Thompson and Hastie (1990): negotiators assume the other side wants the opposite, and miss what both want.
The one about scope creep · Session 37 / 18

Tell the orange story. It takes thirty seconds and people remember it.

Two sisters want the same orange. They argue, then split it down the middle. One squeezes her half for juice and throws away the peel. The other grates her half's peel into a cake and throws away the fruit. If either had asked the other what she wanted it for, both could have had all of what they needed.

The story goes back to Mary Parker Follett, and Fisher and Ury use it in Getting to Yes. The research behind it is from Leigh Thompson and Reid Hastie. They found that negotiators walk in assuming the other side wants the opposite of what they want. Many never notice the issues where both sides want the same thing, and they settle for worse deals than they could have had.

Hold on to that. The next slide is what it means for a scope conversation.

Part 2 · Push back as a partner

On a retainer

Same team, same fee. The more each month delivers, the stronger the case to keep working together.

The one about scope creep · Session 38 / 18

The most important slide in the talk. Slow down here.

Scope conversations look like the orange. The client wants the feature. We want the date. Those look opposed, so both sides start splitting the difference: half the feature, a slightly later date, nobody happy.

But look at what each side wants underneath. The client wants the date too. Their launch hangs off it, and their sponsor has already promised it to someone above them. We want the date because it's the proof clients remember, and the reason they hire us for the next phase. Both sides want the problem solved. That's the overlap, and it's bigger than it looks from either side.

Know where our side stands. On a fixed price, a late week costs us money, so we have every reason to go fast. On a retainer, the client pays the same fee for the same team every month, and the more that team delivers, the stronger the case to keep working together. Slowing down never pays us. This is for you to know, not a line for the client. They see it in how you work: steady delivery, progress shown every week, and a pushback that always comes with a way to keep the date. That is what makes a pushback read as protecting the date.

Then go looking for the version where both sides win. Often the feature has a smaller version that solves the actual problem and keeps the date. The weekly summary email instead of the full export. Three fixed reports instead of a dashboard builder. You only find it by asking what the ask is for.

So when you push back, you aren't defending our side. You are looking for the deal where both sides get what they need.

Part 3 · Make the case

Start with no, then open the conversation.

They sayCan we add a bulk export? Should be quick.
You say

Not in this scope. Tell me what it’s for, and I’ll come back tomorrow with what it would take.

Why it works37signals, Getting Real, “Start With No”: “Each time you say yes to a feature, you’re adopting a child.” Steve Jobs, Fortune (2008): focus means “saying no to the hundred other good ideas.”
The one about scope creep · Session 39 / 18

The core move. Say the reply out loud, slowly.

Part three: what that looks like on an ordinary call. We'll follow one ask from the moment it arrives. A sponsor says, in passing, at the end of a call: can we add a bulk export, it should be quick.

The habit is to start with no. Not a closed no. A no to the current scope, followed straight away by an open question. "Not in this scope. Tell me what it's for, and I'll come back tomorrow with what it would take."

Look at what that reply does. It doesn't commit you on the spot, which is when you are most likely to guess low. It says the boundary out loud, so nobody later thinks this was always included. And it moves straight to the client's problem, which is the next slide.

The people who build good products say the same thing. The 37signals team called a chapter of Getting Real "Start With No". Their line is that each time you say yes to a feature, you're adopting a child: you carry it through design, build and testing, and then you're stuck with it. Steve Jobs told Fortune that focus means saying no to the hundred other good ideas.

Starting with no buys you the time for a good conversation.

“Not one of those million men wanted the drills. They wanted quarter inch holes.” Advertisement, 1942, via Quote Investigator
The one about scope creep · Session 310 / 18

You are the expert in the room. Say the asks are made up.

Back to the bulk export. You asked what it's for, and the answer is the first row: finance matches orders by hand every Monday. Clients often arrive with a solution like this. "Add a bulk export." "We need a full audit log." That's natural: they're describing the fix they can picture. But you are the expert on what software can do, and their first solution is often not the best one. So ask for the problem underneath, and bring the solution yourself.

There's an old line in marketing: people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole. It's often credited to Theodore Levitt, but it first appears in a 1942 advert. Programmers call the same thing the XY problem: asking about your attempted solution instead of your actual problem.

These asks are made up, but each is typical. Once you know the problem, a smaller change often solves it. For the export, a weekly summary email does the job in a day. Sometimes something already in scope solves it. Sometimes the real problem is bigger than the ask, and the client needs to hear that too.

The questions are simple. What happens today? Who does it affect? What changes when it's solved? How will we know it worked?

Part 3 · Make the case

Ask what it’s worth.

About one idea in three improved the metric it was built to move.

“It is humbling to see how bad experts are at estimating the value of features.”

Ronny Kohavi, Roger Longbotham and others, “Online Experimentation at Microsoft”, 2009
The one about scope creep · Session 311 / 18

It's fair to ask. Frame it as helping the product owner.

Once you know the problem, ask the product owner what solving it is worth. More revenue, lower cost, a risk removed, a deadline met. A rough number is enough. For the export, it's six hours of finance time a week, and a month-end close that stops running late. Then set it against what the change costs. That turns a scope argument into a prioritisation conversation.

Ronny Kohavi ran experiments at Microsoft for years. Every feature they tested was one somebody believed in. Only about one in three improved the metric it was designed to improve. In his words, "It is humbling to see how bad experts are at estimating the value of features." When they first shared the numbers inside Microsoft, many people dismissed them.

So the value of an ask is a guess, even when the person asking is sure. Asking for the guess isn't a challenge. It helps the product owner make the case to their own sponsor. Don Reinertsen puts it as "If you only quantify one thing, quantify the Cost of Delay." Ask what happens if it waits a month. If the answer is "not much", it can wait for the next milestone.

The one about scope creep · Session 312 / 18

The AI part. Expect this objection on every call now.

Back to the bulk export. Before you send the page, someone will say it, the client or one of us: you use AI, isn't that a couple of hours?

The code might take a couple of hours. Everything around it doesn't shrink the same way. Someone reviews it. Someone writes and runs the tests. It goes through release. Someone answers the support question when it breaks, and it needs looking after for the life of the system. Getting Real has a chapter on this called Hidden Costs.

And our sense of how fast we are is shaky. In 2025, METR ran a controlled trial with sixteen experienced developers, each working on an open-source project they knew well. Before starting, they forecast that AI would make them 24% faster. Measured, they were 19% slower. Afterwards, they still believed AI had made them 20% faster. METR is careful to say this doesn't hold for every developer or task. The lesson for us is narrower: the feeling of speed isn't a measurement.

This came up in the questions last time too. Agents make asks look small, and sometimes doing a half-hour change overnight is the right call. The client asked on Tuesday and had it on Wednesday, and that's a story they'll tell. Do it when it's worth it. But it still goes on the log, which comes right after the one-page note.

The one about scope creep · Session 313 / 18

For everyone on a retainer. The list on the right is made up.

Now run the same ask on a retainer. The bulk export is four days of work, and the conversation is different, in some ways easier. Our retainers are a fixed team for a fixed monthly fee. We don't count hours. So the client doesn't pay more for a new ask. The cost shows up somewhere else: something already planned for the month doesn't happen.

The trap is that nobody sees it. Each new ask slides in, the planned work slides out, and at the end of the quarter the outcomes we agreed haven't landed. Nobody can point to the moment it went wrong.

So keep the month's list visible, with sizes, and draw the line where the team's month runs out. When a new ask arrives, the answer is yes, and here's the list. Which one moves to next month? The client chooses. The line stays where it is.

This is how Basecamp runs product work. They call it fixed time, variable scope. Beck and Cleal proposed the same thing for contracts in 1999: fix the time, the cost and the quality, and let scope absorb the uncertainty. Their example contract was a team of six, paid by the month. That's our retainer. The model only works if the scope trade happens out loud, every time.

The payoff of Part 3. Everything from the last four slides lands on this page. Say the example is made up.

Back on the fixed-price project, here's where the bulk export ends up: one page, written the same day. It's the change request note from the handbook, artifact A4. The example is made up, but every line comes from the conversation we just followed.

Read it from the bottom up first, because that's the order you collected it in. The ask, in the sponsor's words. The problem behind it: finance matching orders by hand every Monday. What it's worth: six hours a week and a month-end close that stops running late. What it costs, all of it, not only the code. Agents can write a feature in an afternoon now, but review, tests, release, documents and upkeep still take the time they took.

Then the options, including the smaller version you found by asking what it was for: a weekly summary email, one day of work.

Now look at the order on the page. The recommendation sits on top, with the decision and the name of the person who made it. Barbara Minto called this the pyramid principle: the answer first, the reasons under it. Your sponsor has two minutes. They want to know what you suggest and what they're being asked to decide. The detail is there if they want it.

The name matters. A decision without a name gets re-opened when the sponsor changes. Send it when you need a decision from the client. When you don't, write it anyway: it keeps our side of the road clean, and over the project these pages show every yes, every no and every swap. If it needs more than a page, the ask is big enough to be its own milestone.

The one about scope creep · Session 315 / 18

Added after the first run. The room asked for this one.

The one-page note is for asks that need a decision. Most asks don't. They're small, you say yes, and they're done by the next morning. Those are the ones to watch, because they add up. We've seen it on our own accounts: lots of small overnight extras, each one nothing, and together they become weeks.

So log every yes, including the free ones. The ask, what it would have cost, what you decided, and what you absorbed. When you absorb one, tell the client it was outside the plan. You aren't asking for credit. You're keeping the record straight.

The log does three things. On the day an ask tips you over, you can point to it: here's everything we said yes to beyond the plan, and this one needs a decision. It shows the client you don't say no all the time: wherever it was reasonable, you did it. And put it in the weekly update, so the client sees what they got on top of what they paid for.

Without it, a habit of yes becomes an expectation of yes. At the end of the project, this log and the estimate-against-actual sheet from session two answer "could you have been faster?" before anyone asks.

Five phrases to retire, and what to say instead.

The one about scope creep · Session 316 / 18

Read the left column in the voice people use on calls. Then the right.

Part four is about words. Here are five phrases we all say on calls, and what to say instead.

"Sure, we'll squeeze it in." There is no squeezing. The work goes somewhere, usually into the last two weeks of the project. Say what it moves.

"Should be quick." This is a guess, made on the spot, before anyone has thought about tests or release. Say you'll size all of it and come back tomorrow.

"That's out of scope." This one is true, but it closes the conversation. The client hears no and stops telling you what they need. Add the question: what's it for?

"We'll try to fit it." This promises something without saying what it costs. Give options instead, with your pick first.

"No problem." Sometimes it is no problem. But if it changes the plan, someone should sign off on that change. Ask who.

The one about scope creep · Session 317 / 18

Added after the first run, from the discussion. End the talk here.

Last slide before questions, and it came from the room the first time I gave this talk. If you work with a client every day, you get close to them. Saying no to a friend is hard, and if you're always the one saying "no, but", it gets tiring for both sides.

So you don't have to be the only one. Let your lead be the bad cop sometimes: "I'd love to do this. Let me check with my lead whether we can fit it." That keeps you on the client's side of the table, and it gives the ask a day to settle.

And no has more steps than one. A rule of thumb from consulting has four Ds. Deny it at first. Defer it to someone who decides. Delay it if the timing is wrong. And if it still matters after all that, do it, written up as a change request. By then there's been enough conversation that the change request surprises nobody.

The one thing never to do is dismiss the ask. Even when you can see straight away that it won't help, take it in, think about it, and come back with a reasoned answer.

The date is ours to keep, so every change to scope gets agreed out loud, with the client, as partners.
