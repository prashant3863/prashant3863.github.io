For Sales and TechPrashant Mittal, realfast

About twenty minutes. Sales and Tech together. Open with the title.

The title is borrowed from The Godfather. An offer you can't refuse is one with no room to argue. That is what we want from an estimate: not the lowest number, and not the one the client hoped for, but one that is so clearly reasoned that neither side wants to argue with it.

Today is about the weeks before signature, when Sales and Tech put a number on work nobody fully understands yet. On many of our projects that number turns out wrong, and we find out in month three, when it is expensive for both sides.

Four parts. Why estimates miss, even good ones. Four pitfalls that make them miss more. How to estimate well, step by step. And how to turn the estimate into a contract.

Most of this comes from outside realfast: estimation research by Steve McConnell and Magne Jørgensen, Bent Flyvbjerg's work on overruns, and forecasting practice from agile teams. Sources are on the last slide.

1 · Why estimates miss

When you write the proposal, the real cost can be anywhere from a quarter to four times your number.

Source: Steve McConnell, Software Estimation (2006), after Barry Boehm (1981). Best-case ranges for skilled estimators.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech02 / 18

Walk the cone left to right. Stop on the shaded band.

This is the cone of uncertainty. Barry Boehm first drew it in 1981, and Steve McConnell made it famous. It shows how far off a good estimate can be at each stage of a project.

At the initial concept, the real cost can land anywhere from a quarter of your estimate to four times it. That is a sixteen-fold range, and it is the best case, for skilled estimators. Once the product is defined, it narrows to half to double. Only when requirements are complete does it get to about plus or minus fifty percent.

Now look at the shaded band. That is where we write proposals: somewhere between the first call and a defined product. We are estimating in the widest part of the cone.

So the first lesson is about honesty, not technique. At this stage, a single number is not an estimate. It is a guess with false precision. Everything in the rest of the talk is about working with that range instead of hiding it.

1 · Why estimates miss

The average overrun is 27%. The real risk is the one project in six.

Figures: Bent Flyvbjerg and Alexander Budzier, 1,471 IT projects, Harvard Business Review (2011). Curve shape is illustrative.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech03 / 18

The point: errors are not symmetric. They lean late.

Bent Flyvbjerg and Alexander Budzier at Oxford studied 1,471 IT projects. The average cost overrun was 27 percent. But one project in six was what they call a black swan: on average 200 percent over budget and 70 percent late.

Notice the shape. Projects can't finish much earlier than planned, but they can finish very much later. The error is lopsided. That matters for how you estimate: if you take your most likely number and quote it, you will be late more often than early, every time.

And you cannot fix the tail by padding every quote. Nobody can add 200 percent to every price. What you can do is spot the work that lives in the tail, and estimate it differently. That is part three.

1 · Why estimates miss

An estimate, a target and a commitment are three different numbers.

When the target falls outside the range, change the scope or the date. Don’t change the estimate.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech04 / 18

The vocabulary slide. Most estimation arguments are about the wrong word.

Steve McConnell makes a distinction that clears up most estimation arguments. There are three different numbers, and we usually use one word for all of them.

The estimate is what the work will probably take. It is a range, here eleven to twenty-one weeks, most likely fourteen. It belongs to the people doing the work.

The target is what the business wants. Here, week ten, because there is a launch event. Targets are legitimate. They belong to the client.

The commitment is what we promise. Week eighteen, for an agreed scope. It is a decision, taken knowing the estimate and the target.

Here the target sits outside the estimate. That is a normal situation, not a crisis. It is the start of a conversation about scope: what can we deliver by week ten? What gets cut, or moved to a second release? What you must not do is quietly change the estimate to match the target. That moves the problem to month three.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech05 / 18

The most important research in this talk. Let the numbers land.

Magne Jørgensen at Simula in Norway has spent twenty years studying how software people estimate. In one experiment, he gave two groups of professionals the same specification. One group was told, in passing, that the client thought it would take about 50 hours. The other was told the client thought 1,000 hours. Both groups were told that the client knew nothing about software, and that they should ignore the number.

The median estimates were 77 hours and 632 hours. Same work, eight times apart. And when asked, the professionals said the client's number had not influenced them.

This is anchoring. Any number you hear first pulls your estimate towards it: the client's budget, a competitor's quote, what the salesperson hopes for, last year's similar deal.

The fix is simple and a little uncomfortable. Tech estimates before hearing the budget. Then compare the estimate with the budget, openly. If they are far apart, that's a scope conversation, which is slide eight.

For Sales: keep asking for the budget. It is essential for shaping scope. Just don't pass it to the estimators until they've written their number down.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech06 / 18

Optional: run three quiz questions with the room first.

McConnell's book opens with a quiz. Ten general-knowledge questions, like the surface temperature of the sun or the year a famous person was born. For each one, give a range you are 90 percent confident contains the answer. If you do it right, you should get nine out of ten.

The average score is 2.8 out of ten. People make their ranges far too narrow, even when there's no reason to. Nobody is judging them, and they are explicitly told to be 90 percent sure.

McConnell's point is that the pressure to look precise comes mostly from ourselves. A wide range feels like admitting we don't know. But at proposal stage, we don't know. The cone said so.

A practical test: would you bet a month's pay that the real number lands inside your range? If not, widen it until you would. A range of ten to twenty weeks that holds is far more useful than "twelve weeks" that doesn't.

Read three or four items aloud. Ask the room which ones they've missed before.

When engineers estimate, they estimate what they picture: building the features. That part is often estimated quite well. The trouble is everything else a project needs.

McConnell lists omitted activities as one of the most common sources of estimation error. Look at the checklist. Getting environments and access. Integrating with the client's systems, which is never as simple as the docs say. Testing and fixing bugs. A security review. Data migration. Deployment. The weekly demo and update. Supporting the client's UAT. Waiting for client inputs. Handover documents. And managing the whole thing.

The proportions on the bar are illustrative, but on many projects this "everything else" is close to half the effort. If your estimate is a list of features with days next to them, it is missing it.

The fix: keep this checklist next to every estimate, and add a line for each item, even if the line says zero. A zero you decided on is fine. A zero you forgot is an overrun.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech08 / 18

The Sales slide. This is where most estimates actually break.

The fourth pitfall is the one we feel most. The client says "can you do it in eight weeks?" The deal is close. And someone says "we'll make it work."

What just happened is that the estimate changed, but the work didn't. Flyvbjerg has a name for the pattern at scale: the planning fallacy, which is honest optimism, and strategic misrepresentation, which is shaving a number to win. Either way, the gap between the work and the time doesn't disappear. It moves to month three, where it is a problem for both sides: we lose margin, and the client gets a late or rushed delivery.

The alternative is to negotiate the scope, not the estimate. "Here's what fits in eight weeks, and here's what moves to phase two." Now the trade-off is visible, and the client makes it, which is where that decision belongs.

This is easier when the estimate is a range with reasons behind it, which is part three. It is hard to argue with a well-reasoned range. It is easy to haggle over a single number.

The core move. Small pieces, then three bins.

Step one is to break the work into pieces small enough to reason about. A rule of thumb: no piece bigger than about two weeks of one person's work. Big pieces hide surprises. Include the forgotten-work checklist from slide seven as pieces too.

Then sort each piece into one of three bins. Known: you have built it before, or you could write the acceptance test today. Give it a tight range.

Assumed: you believe it, but nobody has checked. The API exists and does what we think. The data is clean. Give it a range, and write the assumption down, with what happens if it's false. Those assumptions go into the proposal.

Unknown: nobody can answer yet, not us and not the client. Don't estimate these at all. Any number would be invented. Give them a timeboxed spike instead: "three days to find out how the old system handles refunds", then estimate after.

Look at the bars. We usually estimate everything the same way, as if it were all known. In reality, part of it is assumed and part unknown. The proportions here are illustrative, but try it on your last estimate.

3 · How to estimate

Step two: give every piece a best, likely and worst case. The likely case is not the average.

expected = (best + 4 × likely + worst) ÷ 6

Add up the expected values, not the likely ones.

Three-point (PERT) estimation. Example task is illustrative.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech10 / 18

One formula. Show why it matters with the example.

For every piece, ask three questions. If everything goes well, how long? What's most likely? And if things go wrong, the realistic worst case, not the apocalypse?

Here's one task. Best case ten days. Most likely fifteen. Worst case thirty-five. Notice the shape again: it can't go much faster than ten, but it can go a lot slower. Remember the long tail.

The classic three-point formula, from PERT, weights it: best plus four times likely plus worst, divided by six. Here, that is 17.5 days, not fifteen. The long right side pulls the expected value up.

That is the point of the slide. If you add up the "most likely" numbers for fifty tasks, you will be late, because each one is a little optimistic and they add up. Add up the expected values instead.

A bonus: when someone gives you a worst case three times the likely case, that's a signal. That task probably belongs in the assumed or unknown bin.

3 · How to estimate

Step three: estimate alone first, then talk about the biggest gaps.

Three to six people, at least one who will build it. Sales joins after the number is written down.

Wideband Delphi, after Barry Boehm (1981). Values are illustrative.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech11 / 18

The gaps are the value, not the average.

One person's estimate carries one person's blind spots. The fix is a method called Wideband Delphi, which Barry Boehm popularised. Planning poker is a lighter version of the same idea.

Three to six people. At least one of them should be someone who will actually build it. Each person estimates alone, in writing, so nobody anchors on the most senior voice. That's pitfall one again.

Then look at the spread. In round one here, it runs from six to twenty-eight weeks. Don't average it. Ask the highest and the lowest to explain. Almost always, they're imagining different work. One assumed the client's API is ready. The other has integrated with that kind of system before and knows it isn't. That conversation is the most valuable thing in the whole estimate. Write down every assumption it surfaces.

Then estimate alone again. The range narrows because people now share the same picture of the work, not because they're pressured to agree. Two or three rounds is usually enough.

Sales joins after the number is written down, for the same reason: no anchoring. Then Sales brings the budget, and you compare.

The numbers here are made up. Say so. The habit is the point.

Everything so far is a bottom-up estimate: you look at this project from the inside. Daniel Kahneman showed that inside views are reliably optimistic. He called it the planning fallacy. The correction, which Flyvbjerg calls reference class forecasting, is the outside view: how did similar projects actually turn out?

We can do this with our own history. One shared sheet. For every finished project: the type, what we sold, what it actually took, and the ratio. When you estimate a new project, multiply the bottom-up total by the typical ratio for that type, or at least explain why this one is different.

The numbers on the slide are illustrative, not ours. But the pattern is common: work you know well runs a little over, and rescues and rewrites run far over.

The sheet doesn't exist yet. Ten real data points would already beat anyone's gut feeling, including mine. That's one of the asks at the end.

3 · How to estimate

Step five: quote a range and a confidence, not a single number.

Say it as: “14 to 18 weeks. We commit to 18, for this scope.”

Probabilistic forecasting, as practised with Monte Carlo simulation (Troy Magennis). Curve is illustrative.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech13 / 18

This is the estimate they can't refuse. A range, a confidence, and the reasons.

Once you have three-point estimates for every piece, you can do something better than adding them up. You can ask: what is the chance of finishing by each date? A simple Monte Carlo simulation in a spreadsheet does this: it plays out the project thousands of times, picking a random duration for each piece within its range, and counts how often you finish by each week. Troy Magennis has free spreadsheets for exactly this.

The result is a curve like this one. Read it like this. By week fourteen, there's a fifty percent chance you're done. That's P50, a coin toss. By week eighteen, eighty-five percent. That's P85.

Most teams quote the P50 without knowing it, because they added up most-likely numbers. That means they're late half the time, by design.

The habit: commit to P85, and say the range out loud. "Fourteen to eighteen weeks. We commit to eighteen, for this scope." That sentence is hard to argue with. It shows you've done the work, it's honest about uncertainty, and it gives the client something firm to plan against.

You don't need the simulation every time. Even the three-point totals give you a rough P50 and P85. The point is to stop pretending a range is a number.

An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech14 / 18

The biggest single move. When estimating can't narrow the range, discovery can.

Sometimes you do everything right, and the range is still too wide to price. Too much is in the unknown bin. That's the cone talking. No estimation technique can fix it, because the information doesn't exist yet.

So buy the information. A short paid discovery, two to three weeks at a fixed fee, moves you along the cone. On McConnell's numbers, from half-to-double at "product defined", to roughly two-thirds to one-and-a-half once requirements are done. That's the difference between a guess and a price.

Discovery hands over five things: what we'll build in handover terms, a Definition of Done both sides sign, the unknowns turned into answers by spikes, an eval set for AI work, and a P85 estimate for the build.

Blair Enns, who advises professional services firms, puts it as: diagnose before you prescribe. Two things make it easy to sell. Credit the discovery fee against the build, so the client pays earlier, not extra. And make the output useful on its own, so they get value whether or not we build it.

Connect the estimate to the commercial model.

The gap between P50 and P85 tells you how much uncertainty is left. Let it pick the contract.

Narrow range, say P85 within about a quarter of P50: fixed price is fine. Any overrun comes out of our margin, but it's a small, priced risk. The 25 percent is a rule of thumb, not a law.

A bit wider: fixed price with a checkpoint. This comes from the book Agile Contracts. After the first few sprints, both sides re-estimate with real data, then continue, re-baseline, or exit.

Wider still: target cost, borrowed from construction. Agree a target. If it comes in over, both sides share the overrun by an agreed ratio. Under, and both share the saving.

Widest: fixed budget, flexible scope. Capped time and materials with a ranked backlog, built from the top.

A fixed price on a wide range isn't a price. It's a bet. And when it goes wrong, it goes wrong for both sides: we lose margin, the client gets a stressed team and a late delivery.

4 · From estimate to contract

You can’t estimate what nobody can list. Rewrites and takeovers need an audit first.

Finish the build. Rewrite it on a modern stack. Make it do what the current system does.
Estimate it in this order

    A fixed-fee audit of the code or the old system.
  2. 02
    Record what it really does, with tests against the running system.
  3. 03
    A parity list both sides sign. That list is the Definition of Done.
  4. 04
    Now estimate, piece by piece, from the list.
An estimate they can’t refuse · Sales and Tech16 / 18

The work that lives in the long tail. It sounds clear and isn't.

Remember the track-record table: rescues and rewrites ran far over. Here's why.

Some scope sounds very clear. "Finish the build." "Rewrite it on a modern stack." "Make it do what the current system does." Everyone nods. But none of these defines done. They point at something else and say "that". Even a vague Definition of Done is better, because it at least tries to say what done means.

Bottom-up estimation needs a list of work. For a rewrite, the users will list the screens and the main flows. That's the tip of the iceberg, and that's what gets estimated. Below the water is everything else the old system does: business rules nobody wrote down, edge cases someone fixed years ago, bugs that people now depend on, reports and nightly feeds, defaults and ordering. The old system is the only complete spec of itself. This is the feature-parity trap.

Hyrum's Law, from a Google engineer, says it well: with enough users, every observable behaviour will be depended on by somebody. Bugs included.

Taking over a half-finished build from another team has the same shape. Nobody knows what finished means, or what state the code is in.

So estimate in this order. A fixed-fee audit first. Record what the old system really does, with tests against it. Turn that into a parity list both sides sign: that's the Definition of Done. Only then estimate, piece by piece, from the list.

To sum up. Estimates miss because of the cone and the long tail, and they miss more because of anchoring, overconfidence, forgotten work and pressure. The fix: break the work down and sort it, three-point every piece, estimate alone then compare, check your track record, and quote a range with a confidence. When the range is too wide, buy information first, and let the width of the range pick the contract.

That's the estimate they can't refuse. Not the lowest number. The one with its reasons showing.
