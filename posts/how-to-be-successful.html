How to be successful at work

Being successful at work isn’t about being the smartest person in the room or the busiest. It’s owning the outcome, saying what you think, and making the people around you better.

How you take on work

Habits 1 to 5
01

Master your craft

  • You know the basics of your field well enough to explain them to someone new.
  • You set aside time each week to get better, and you keep that time when work gets busy.
  • You study work by the best people in your field, then practise until yours gets closer.
  • You ask people who are better than you to review your work, and you act on what they say.
  • You automate the work you keep repeating, so your time goes to the parts that need you.

None of the other habits can make up for weak skills.

Attitude is no substitute for competence.

Eric S. Raymond, How to Become a Hacker

Raymond reckons mastery takes about ten years, in hacking as in many other fields. After that, people who take it seriously spend the rest of their lives “learning and perfecting” their craft. What counts here is the practice.

02

High agency

  • You treat a problem as yours until it’s solved, or until you’ve run out of ways to solve it.
  • You find out who can unblock you, ask them directly, and follow up if nothing moves.
  • When something is unclear, you write down your best understanding, check it, and start on the parts that don’t depend on the answer.
  • You fix what’s broken in front of you, or you tell the person who owns it the same day.
  • You say “blocked” only after you’ve tried the routes you can think of, and you name them.

Most blockers clear with one more message, one more search or one more attempt. High agency doesn’t mean working alone, though. Keep people informed while you work, so nobody is surprised by what you did.

03

Be resourceful

  • You shrink a problem until some part of it can move today.
  • You change the angle when the first route fails: a different person, a smaller test, a tool that does the slow part.
  • You look for how someone else solved it before you invent a new way.
  • You bring options with a problem: what’s wrong, two ways out, and the one you’d pick.
  • If you can’t see a way out yet, you raise the problem anyway. Not having a fix is never a reason to keep quiet.

Resourcefulness is what turns high agency into progress. The Stoic emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote:

What stands in the way becomes the way.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.20, translated by Gregory Hays

A blocker tells you which route is closed and often why, and that points you to the next one.

04

Extreme ownership

  • You own the result, including the parts you didn’t build.
  • When something goes wrong, your first question is “what could I have done differently?”
  • You say what happened, what you’re doing about it and what you’ll change, before anyone asks.
  • When something isn’t your area, you find whose it is and hand it over with the context they need.
  • Once you see a gap, it’s yours until you’ve handed it to someone.

The term comes from Jocko Willink and Leif Babin’s book of the same name. A leader owns everything in their world and blames no one else.

You’re never the victim. Owners assume they can change anything they own. When the cause is outside your control, your response is still yours: what you tell people and what you do next. Stoicism is built on that idea. Epictetus, who was born a slave, opens his handbook by saying your own actions are always in your control. Blaming circumstances leaves you waiting for them to change.

05

Be pragmatic

  • You pick the approach that solves the problem in the time available.
  • You ship the simplest version that works, then improve it with feedback.
  • You match the effort to the stakes. A one-off job doesn’t need the polish of something people will use for years.
  • You use what already exists before you make something new.
  • You say the trade-off out loud: what you’re leaving out, and when it would start to matter.

Something that works by the date it’s needed is worth more than an elegant version that arrives late. Pragmatism applies to scope and approach, though, not to the quality bar in habit 11. You can cut scope, but what you ship still has to work.

How you think and speak

Habits 6 to 9
06

Be curious

  • You want to know how things work, including things outside your job.
  • You learn the whole business you’re part of: who it serves, what they need, and how it makes money.
  • You look for the problem behind a request before you solve the request.
  • You try new tools on real work and share what you learned, including what didn’t work.
  • When something surprises you, you find out why before you move on.

Curious people spot the cheaper fix that others miss, because they understand more of the system than their own part of it.

07

Radical candor

  • You give feedback within a day, while the details are fresh.
  • You’re specific: what happened, what it caused, and what you’d do instead.
  • You criticise in private and praise in public.
  • You say a plan won’t work in the room where it’s discussed, not in a private message afterwards.
  • When you get feedback, you say thanks and ask a question before you defend yourself.

Radical candor is Kim Scott’s name for caring personally and challenging directly at the same time. Kind feedback that skips the hard part leaves the person to find out later, often in front of others. Hard feedback without care makes people defensive, and they stop listening. Scott calls staying quiet to keep things pleasant ruinous empathy. It feels kind and leaves the other person worse off.

08

Asking why is your duty

  • You ask why, including about decisions made above you.
  • You ask to understand first, then challenge.
  • You ask what problem a request solves before you agree to do it.
  • You turn the same questions on your own plans before you present them.
  • Once you’ve been heard and a decision is made, you commit to it and say so.

Four questions do most of the work:

The problem

What problem does this solve, and for whom?

The assumptions

What are we assuming, and how do we know it's true?

The alternatives

What else did we consider, and why not that?

The evidence

What would change your mind?

They come from the Socratic method. Socrates taught by asking questions until people found the gaps in their own reasoning. People believe a flaw they found themselves more than one someone pointed out to them.

Asking why is not a veto. Amazon calls the next step disagree and commit. Once the decision is made, you back it fully, and you raise it again only if new facts come in. If you made the decision, answer every why, even when it’s inconvenient. If you can’t answer one, the decision needs more thought.

09

Sell your thought process

  • You lead with the recommendation, then give the reasoning.
  • You write down the options you considered, the one you picked and why, and what would change your mind.
  • You start from what the other person cares about: cost, risk or time.
  • You do the work to bring people along instead of expecting them to follow.

When you share a conclusion without the reasoning, people can only agree or disagree with you. When you show the reasoning, they can check it, correct one step, or reuse it on their next problem. People don’t adopt an idea they don’t understand, even a good one.

How you work with others

Habits 10 to 13
10

Be professional

  • You do what you said, by when you said. If a date will slip, you say so before it does and give a new one.
  • You’re on time, and you’ve read what was sent before the meeting.
  • You reply within a working day, even if the reply is “I’ll have an answer by Thursday.”
  • You keep confidential information confidential.
  • You don’t criticise people in front of others. You take it to them directly (habit 7).

Professionalism means people can rely on you without checking up on you. People judge a whole team by how each person on it shows up.

11

Hold each other accountable

  • You tell a colleague when they’ve cut a corner, the way you’d want to hear it (habit 7). You don’t need to be their manager to do it.
  • You fix a broken window when you see it, or you log it with an owner and a date.
  • When the easy option and the right one differ, you take the right one. If you can’t, you say why.
  • You review work as if you’ll have to live with it, and you send back what isn’t ready.
  • You expect the same for your work.

The broken windows idea says visible neglect invites more neglect. The Pragmatic Programmer applied it to software: one ignored problem makes the next one easier to ignore. Skipping a check, papering over a problem, or agreeing to something you haven’t thought through all save time today and cost more later.

12

Research, then ask for help

  • You assume someone has solved a problem like yours before, and you look for how.
  • You search, read and try before you ask, with a time limit. If an hour goes by without progress, you ask.
  • Your ask says what you’re trying to do, what you’ve tried, and your best guess at the problem.
  • You make time when someone asks you well.
  • You work in the open, so others can join in and find your work later.

Most problems you’ll meet have been solved before, at least in part, by someone else. Research and a good question are how you find them. You can get this wrong in two ways. If you ask before you’ve tried, you hand someone else your thinking and learn nothing. If you stay stuck for days, you burn time a colleague could have saved you in ten minutes.

13

Mentor, guide and help

  • You make the people around you better at what they do, with or without a title that says so.
  • You explain why when you review someone’s work, not only what to change.
  • When someone brings you a problem, you ask questions that lead them to the answer before you give it (habit 8).
  • You pair on the hard part instead of taking it over.
  • You write up what you learned so the next person doesn’t start from scratch.
  • You bring newer people into conversations they’ll learn from, and talk it over with them afterwards.

At the end of a piece of work, ask yourself who got better because you were part of it.
